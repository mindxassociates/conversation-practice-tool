A useful reframe
The goal is not “make the conversation go perfectly.” The goal is “stay present long enough to make one more move.”

Try this before practice:

Exhale slowly. Drop your shoulders. Put both feet on the floor. Aim for curious, not impressive.
The method

Four moves are enough

Good conversation is usually a loop, not a performance. Use these moves flexibly rather than trying to “say the right thing.”

1

Notice

Use the shared situation: the event, place, task, class, food, weather, or what just happened.

Example: “This place is busier than I expected.”

2

Open

Ask a low-stakes question that fits the context. Open-ended questions make it easier to continue.

Example: “Have you been here before?”

3

Follow the thread

Listen for one detail and ask about that detail instead of jumping to a completely new topic.

Example: “You said you just moved here—what brought you to the area?”

4

Share a little

Conversation works better as an exchange. Add one related sentence about yourself, then hand the conversation back.

Example: “I’m still learning the area too. I’ve been trying different coffee shops—have you found any you like?”

When your mind goes blank

Use FORD-lite: Food, Occupation/school, Recreation, or the shared Details around you. You do not have to cover all four.

“What have you been into lately?”“How do you know the host?”“What do you usually order here?”

What to practice

Ask → listen → pick one detail
Your next question does not need to be clever. It only needs to connect to something they just said.
Use “comment + question”
Example: “That sounds like a lot of work. What part has been the most interesting?”
Let pauses happen
A 1–3 second pause can feel much longer when anxious. You usually do not need to rescue it instantly.
Conversation toolkit

Use these when you need a bridge

These are patterns, not scripts. Change the wording so it sounds like you.

Context openers

“How did you hear about this?”

“What did you think of ___?”

“Have you done this before?”

Interest openers

“What have you been watching or listening to lately?”

“What do you like doing when you actually have free time?”

“Anything you’ve been looking forward to?”

Easy follow-ups

“How did you get into that?”

“What do you like about it?”

“What happened next?”

“Would you recommend it?”

Graceful exits

Ending a conversation is a skill too. Try: “It was good talking with you—I’m going to grab a drink / find my friend / get back to this, but I’m glad we talked.”

If the other person is quiet

Their short answer is not proof you did something wrong. They may be tired, distracted, shy, busy, or simply not in a talking mood. You can make one more gentle attempt, then let the interaction end.

Before you use this in real life
